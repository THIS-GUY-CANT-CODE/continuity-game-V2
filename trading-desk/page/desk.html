What daily trading £1,300 actually pays

You asked for a deep look, so here is the uncomfortable part first. The best large studies of people who day-trade their own money all land in the same place. In Brazil, of everyone who kept day trading for more than 300 days, 97% lost money and 0.4% earned more than a bank teller. In Taiwan, fewer than 1% of day traders earned reliably positive returns after costs over 15 years. The losers were not stupid. They were paying costs on every trade and guessing on direction.

With £1,300 on Trading 212 the costs are not commissions (there are none) but the small percentages that hide in each trade: the 0.15% currency fee each way on anything priced in dollars, the 0.5% stamp duty on every UK share purchase, and the bid-offer spread. Here is what a round trip a day costs over a year, before you have made or lost a penny on direction.

Does buying dips actually work?

I tested the classic short-term dip rules on 33 years of S&P 500 data (a total-return series that mimics an accumulating tracker, 1993 to December 2025), charging 0.05% per side and filling every order at the next close, which is slower than you will manage at the London open. Buy only when the index is above its 200-day average and at least two of four pullback triggers fire; sell on the first close above the 5-day average, after 10 days, or 6% below entry.

Translate that to a £400 sleeve and the honest numbers are: a typical trade makes about £2, a typical year makes £15 to £20, the worst single trade in 33 years would have cost £32, and the deepest losing run £46. While the sleeve waits in cash (89% of the time) Trading 212 pays interest on it, currently around 3.6% a year, which is another £13.

The verdict

You cannot earn an income from £1,300, and anyone who says otherwise is selling something. What you can do is run a professional process on a small account: keep most of it compounding in cheap global trackers, trade a defined sleeve on rules with a measured edge, pay almost nothing in costs, and keep a journal. Do that for a year and the habit is worth far more than the £80 or so the account is likely to make. If you add money monthly, the same machine scales without changing a rule.

The rulebook

Rules you can check with a yes or no, decided the night before, written here so that the morning version of you does not negotiate with them.

Sleeve: buy the dip, sell the bounce

Trend
Only buy when the S&P 500 closed above its 200-day average. Below it, the sleeve stays in cash and earns interest, however tempting the dip.
Entry
At least two of four pullback triggers on the previous US close: RSI(2) below 10 · RSI(4) below 25 · three straight down closes · 2% or more below the 10-day high. Buy £400 of VUAG after 08:05 UK the next morning, limit order at the live offer.
Exit
Sell at the next London open after the first close above the 5-day average. Failing that, sell after 10 trading days. Failing that, sell if the price is 6% below your entry. Whichever comes first.
Size
One position at a time, the whole sleeve, nothing else. The worst realistic loss on one trade is about £25 to £32, under 2.5% of the account.
Stop
If the sleeve is ever 15% (£60) below its best value, it stops for a calendar month. We review the journal together before it restarts.

Core: add on deep dips, never sell

Trigger
Three or more triggers fire, or the index is 4% or more below its 10-day high, with the trend filter on.
Action
Move £60 of the cash buffer into VWRP. The buffer refills from money you add. Core holdings are sold only if you need the money for your life.
The nevers. No CFDs, no leverage, no options. No single stocks in the first 90 days (after that, at most £50 of play money, separate from the sleeve, and written down as tuition). No averaging down. No adding to a position because it "feels" oversold. No trades that were not in the morning brief. No checking the app more than twice a day.

Your morning routine

Five minutes before the London open. Trading 212 shows the ETFs live from 08:00; the first five minutes are an auction with wide spreads, so act after 08:05.

  1. 06:42 UK, weekdays
    The brief lands as a push notification and this page updates. It reads the previous US close, runs the rules, and tells you BUY, SELL, HOLD or CASH, with the exact amount.
  2. 08:05 to 08:30
    Do exactly what the brief says, nothing else. BUY: £400 of VUAG, limit at the live offer. SELL: all VUAG units. CASH or HOLD: close the app.
  3. Right after
    Tell me the fill in one line ("bought £400 VUAG at 110.95"). I record it so tomorrow's brief tracks the real position and P&L.
  4. Monthly
    If you add money, say how much. It tops up the cash buffer first, then the core. Once a quarter we look at the journal: win rate, average trade, and whether you followed the rules.

How the daily update works

A scheduled task wakes a fresh Claude session every weekday at 06:42 UK. It fetches the latest S&P 500 close and the ETF quotes, runs the desk script against the saved portfolio state, writes the day's brief to the trading-desk branch of your repository, splices it into this page, and sends you a push notification. The rules, the backtester and the brief generator are all in that branch so you can read and change them.

  • Live prices. Your cloud environment currently blocks market-data hosts, so the session finds the closes by web search, which is slower and occasionally a day behind. To fix that, open the environment's network settings and add query1.finance.yahoo.com and query2.finance.yahoo.com to Allowed domains, keeping package managers ticked.
  • Execution stays with you. Trading 212 has a beta API that can place orders, but I have deliberately not wired it up. A human tapping Buy on a rule they understand is the safety mechanism in this plan. We can revisit after three clean months.
  • Pausing. Ask me to pause or delete the scheduled task at any time. Nothing trades without you.
